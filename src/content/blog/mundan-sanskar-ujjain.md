There is a photograph in nearly every Indian family album: a solemn, freshly shaved baby in a grandparent's arms, wearing the expression of someone who has just survived history. In many homes that first haircut happened in a salon. In millions of others it happened at a tirtha, as a sacrament. **Mundan sanskar in Ujjain** belongs to the second kind — families travel here with their child to perform the **chudakarana sanskar** on the banks of the Shipra, within the embrace of the city of Mahakal.

If you are planning one, here is the rite itself, the logistics around it, and a gentle way to pace the day.

## What Chudakarana Means

Among the sixteen sanskars of tradition, chudakarana — the mundan — is the first haircut of childhood. The hair a child is born with is not discarded but offered: tradition reads it as carrying the imprint of lives before this one, and its removal as a cleansing, the marking of the child's true beginning in this family, this name, this home. The rite is commonly done in the first, third or fifth year per family custom — and, as with everything in the tradition, on a day fixed by the panchang rather than by convenience.

Beyond the theology, every parent knows the practical truth: the hair often grows back thicker, and the day becomes the first story the child is told about themselves. It is a family festival in miniature — which is exactly how it deserves to be treated.

## Why Families Choose Ujjain

A mundan becomes a yatra when it meets a river and a temple, and in Ujjain it meets both. The **Shipra at Ram Ghat** receives the snaan that follows the shaving, and **Mahakaleshwar** receives the darshan that closes the day. Families arrive from across Madhya Pradesh and beyond — parents, child, grandparents, sometimes a vanful of cousins — so the sanskar doubles as a reunion. The child, of course, remains unimpressed by all of it, which is as it should be.

## The Vidhi at Ram Ghat

The **mundan ceremony at Ram Ghat** is short, warm and matter-of-fact, in the way of things done by people who have done them for generations:

- The family priest or a ghat panda takes a **sankalp** in the name of the child
- A hereditary barber — the naais of the ghat have shaved generations of heads — shaves the head, keeping a tuft if the family follows that custom
- The hair is ritually offered or respectfully disposed of, never simply swept away
- The child is bathed in the Shipra with the parents close by
- The family proceeds for darshan, and the day closes with bhojan or daan as the priest advises

Bring your own towel and a change of clothes for the child. The barbers provide the rest, and a small pre-agreed dakshina keeps the occasion graceful.

## Choosing the Auspicious Day

Tradition favours months like **Magh and Shravan** for the mundan, but the honest answer is that the right day depends on the child's birth details and your family's sampradaya — some fix it after the first birthday, some in the third year, some by a specific reading of the chart. Do not take a Gregorian date from any website, including ours; ask your family priest and check the current-year panchang. ## Worries Parents Ask About

Two questions reach our desk every week in the season. Is the razor safe? The ghat barbers shave more children in a month than a city salon sees in a year, and it is perfectly acceptable to ask for a fresh blade, which they routinely use. And what if the child cries through it? The child will cry — briefly, loudly, and then not at all. Grandmothers say the crying carries the rite to the ancestors, and there is real comfort in that reading: take the day as it comes, not as it must look in the album.

## A Gentle Day Plan With a Baby

The mistake families make is treating a sanskar day like a sightseeing day. The child decides how the day goes; everything else is negotiation. A rhythm that works:

- The mundan and snaan in the early morning, when the ghat steps are cool and the crowds thin

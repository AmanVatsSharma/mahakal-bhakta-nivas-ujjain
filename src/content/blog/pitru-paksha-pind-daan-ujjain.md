There is a kind of journey that produces no photographs for the family album: an elder travelling to a river, alone or with one grown child, to offer water to a parent who is no longer there. **Pind daan in Ujjain** is made of such journeys. Every year, in the fortnight called **Pitru Paksha**, families arrive at Ram Ghat on the Shipra to honour their ancestors — a daughter for her father, a son for his mother, a granddaughter completing a wish that was left half-spoken.

This is what the ritual is, how it works at Ram Ghat, and how to plan it with care, especially when the traveller is elderly.

## Why a River, and Why This One

In Hindu tradition, rites for the departed belong at tirthas — crossings, places where the everyday and the eternal are believed to touch. Gaya is the most famous of these, but **Ujjain is a classic ground for pind daan and tarpan**, and Ram Ghat, with its long stone steps, its lamps and its pandas, is where the city performs it. A river makes the offering thinkable: what is given to the water is carried away. For families, that matters more than any theology.

## What Pind Daan Actually Is

Strip away the ceremony and the ritual is heartbreakingly simple:

- A **pind** — a ball of cooked rice mixed with black sesame, honey and sometimes barley — is prepared for the ancestors
- Offerings are made for **three generations** of the family line, with water and kusha grass
- **Tarpan** accompanies it: water poured with sesame while the names of the departed are spoken aloud
- A **shraddh** often closes the day with bhojan for priests or the poor

The names matter more than the materials. The priest will ask for the departed's name and gotra, and it is the speaking of that name at the river that many families describe as the goodbye a hospital never quite allowed. Devotees believe the offering brings peace to the departed and resolves debts of gratitude the living carry; tradition frames it as both duty and release.

## The Fortnight of Pitru Paksha

**Pitru Paksha** is the sixteen-day lunar fortnight that closes on the Bhadrapada Amavasya — **Sarva Pitru Amavasya**, the main day, when the ghats are at their fullest and the river at its most patient. Families who cannot travel in the fortnight observe tarpan on the **monthly Amavasya** through the rest of the year, and Ram Ghat receives them every time. Because the calendar is lunar, never fix a date from memory or from a website — check the current-year panchang, or ask our desk to confirm the day before you book travel.

## The Pandas of Ram Ghat

The pandas of the ghat are hereditary priests; many of the families serving there today guided the grandfathers of the people who now return to them. Ours is not to recommend one lineage over another, but two practical notes serve every family well. First, agree the samagri list and the dakshina at the start, so the vidhi stays about the ancestors rather than negotiation. Second, tell the priest plainly if the elder performing the rites has trouble standing or sitting — the vidhi can be adjusted, and at a ghat that sees thousands of elders every year, it usually is. Our desk regularly introduces staying families to pandas we have known for years and explains the sequence beforehand.

## How the Morning Goes

The vidhi itself is unhurried but not long — most families spend an hour or two at the ghat with the priest, from the first sankalp to the last tarpan. Arrive in the early morning, when the steps are cool and the light lies gently on the water; by mid-morning the busier ghats belong to the crowds. The priest guides every step: what to hold, when to pour, when to speak the name, when to be silent. You do not need to memorise anything before coming — you need only to carry the names, and the intention, which you already do.

## What to Bring

- The **names of the ancestors** — father, grandfather, great-grandfather, and of the mother's side where family custom includes it
- The family **gotra**, if known; the priest can work around it if it is not
- Plain white or light clothing
- A photograph, if you wish — carried in the pocket or in the heart
- For elderly travellers: medicines, a walking stick, and unhurried time

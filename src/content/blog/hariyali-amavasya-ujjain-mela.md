Most new moon nights pass quietly in temple towns — a thinner crowd, an earlier night. **Hariyali Amavasya in Ujjain** breaks that rule completely. Falling in the lush heart of the monsoon, this **Shravan Amavasya** turns the city into a mela: enormous numbers of devotees come down to the Shipra for snaan, the ghats wear rows of stalls, and the whole affair works as the opening ceremony for the Shravan season that follows. Greenery, a swollen river, temple bells and a city at its fullest — that is the day in one line.

Here is how to read the day, and how to move through it without losing either the family or your good humour.

## An Amavasya That Behaves Like a Festival

Amavasya, the new moon, is normally a day of quiet observance — tarpan for ancestors, modest crowds. But the Amavasya that falls in Shravan is different across much of north and central India, and in Ujjain it is one of the big mela days of the calendar. The name **Hariyali** — greenery — says why: it is the new moon of the monsoon, when the city is washed green and the Shipra runs deep and full. A river at its best invites the city to it, and the city answers.

## The Shipra Snaan

From first light, the ghats fill. **Hariyali Amavasya Shipra snaan** is the heart of the day — tradition holds that a dip on this Shravan new moon carries special merit, and devotees arrive in waves through the morning, from families in matching buses to sadhus who walk in for the season. The river in monsoon is at its deepest, so bathe at the recognised ghats where the steps and the current are known, follow the local crowd rather than finding a private bend, and keep children in hand at the water line. By mid-morning the larger ghats are dense; by afternoon the mood shifts from bathing to milling.

## Inside the Mela

The mela spreads back from the ghats into the old city lanes:

- Stalls of sweets, toys, bangles, rudraksha and pictures of deities, henna and sugar-cane juice
- Processions and bhajans on loudspeakers, with impromptu dancing breaking out in unlikely places
- Lost-children announcements and mela policing — real, and worth knowing about before you need them

Go expecting a river of people, not a stroll. That is not a warning but the character of the thing; a mela that was empty would not be a mela.

## Food, Stalls and Small Pleasures

Half the mela is eaten. The stalls near the ghats do brisk trade in the monsoon classics — roasted corn with lemon and salt, hot pakoras against the drizzle, sugar-cane juice, jalebi straight from the karahi — alongside toys, bangles, bamboo flutes and pictures of deities that will hang in faraway homes by next week. Let the children pick one thing each; the negotiation is part of the memory. And for families who want a proper meal rather than mela food, our kitchen serves pure veg thalis through the day, a short walk from the ghat cluster.

## The Door It Opens: Shravan Somvar

Hariyali Amavasya is, above all, the overture. In the days that follow come the **Shravan Somvars** — the Mondays of Shravan, when the crowds at Mahakaleshwar grow week on week until the month closes. If you are planning a trip around the season, our [Shravan Somvar guide](/blog/shravan-somvar-mahakaleshwar-guide/) covers the Mondays in detail, and the [Ujjain festival days guide](/blog/ujjain-festival-days-guide/) places this day in the full calendar of the year.

## A Softer Way to Do the Day

Not everyone needs the peak of it. The snaan of the day belongs to first light, and the ghats are at their kindest between dawn and about eight in the morning — cooler air, thinner crowds, the river still silver. If you are travelling with elders or small children, do the snaan early, come back for breakfast and a nap at the room, and treat the evening mela as decoration rather than obligation. The festival has no single mandatory hour; it has a mood, and the mood can be met gently.

## Planning for the Crowd

- **Arrive the night before** if you can — our check-in from 12 PM also suits early arrivals on the day itself
- **Snaan at first light** is the classic move: cooler, thinner, and out in time for breakfast
- **Expect road closures** and diverted traffic in the old city; parking across the quarter is scarce, which is when parking at your own dharamshala is worth its weight in gold
- **Fix a meeting point** in case the group splits; phone networks jam near the ghats
- **Dress light, carry water**, and wear sandals that survive wet stone steps
- **Retreat in the middle of the day** — the mistake is trying to power through. Come back to a room near the temple quarter for food and rest, then go out again for the evening

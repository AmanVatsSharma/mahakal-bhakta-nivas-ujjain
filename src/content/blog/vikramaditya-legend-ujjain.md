Every traditional calendar hanging in a Ujjain shop carries a quiet tribute to one king. Beside the familiar year sits a second, larger number — the **Vikram Samvat** year, counted from an era traditionally dated to 57 BCE. The emperor behind that number is **Vikramaditya of Ujjain**, and no ruler anywhere is remembered quite the way this city remembers him: not as a forgotten name in a textbook, but as a living presence ticking inside time itself.

The story of **King Vikramaditya** — his history, his throne and his eternal calendar — is the story of Ujjain at its most golden. And unlike most ancient kings, you can still walk through his capital tomorrow morning.

## The king who made justice a legend

Tradition holds that Vikramaditya ruled from Ujjain, the ancient Avantika, and that his reign became the Indian shorthand for perfect justice. The stories are told in almost every household: the king who walked his city in disguise to see its truth with his own eyes, the judge who weighed the complaint of a poor widow with the same care as a merchant's gold, the ruler whose door was never truly closed.

Historians continue to debate which historical ruler the legends point toward — several kings across the centuries carried the honorific, and the stories grew richer with each retelling. But in Ujjain the question feels settled. This was **his capital**, and the city speaks of him the way a family speaks of a revered ancestor: with pride, with stories, with complete ownership.

## The throne and the thirty-two statues

The **Simhasan Battisi** story cycle tells of the lost throne of Vikramaditya, discovered generations after his passing and guarded by **thirty-two statues** — celestial spirits who keep watch over the seat of power. When a later king tries to claim it, one statue after another demands that he first prove himself worthy, each recounting a virtue of Vikramaditya: his justice, his courage, his generosity toward poets, his refusal to bow to the mighty.

It is a brilliant literary device — thirty-two doors into one character. The message survives because it is uncomfortable: the throne was never furniture. It was a standard of rule, and no successor could sit on it casually. Generations of listeners have understood the point — power is loaned only to those who earn it.

## Baital Pachisi: riddles in the dark

Every Indian child half-knows the **Baital Pachisi** — the vetala tales. King Vikramaditya carries a speaking spirit on his back through a haunted forest, and at the end of every journey the vetala poses a riddle of tangled ethics. Answer, and the spirit escapes back to its tree; stay silent, and it remains captive. Twenty-five times the king must choose — and every choice tests him.

That this cycle is tied to Ujjain's legendary king matters. It sketches the ruler tradition wanted: a man whose justice was tested in the dark, not only in the courtroom. It is fitting that his city belongs to **Mahakal, the lord of time** — the king remembered here is the one who measured his days rightly. ## Vikram Samvat: the calendar that never stopped

Here is what makes Vikramaditya unique among legendary kings: **his calendar is still running.** The Vikram Samvat era, traditionally starting in 57 BCE, remains a civilizational clock in daily use:

- **Panchangs and temple calendars** across India print the Vikram year first
- **Festivals** — Deepavali, Navratri, Ekadashis — are fixed by its months
- The traditional **new year** is welcomed in the Kartik month of the Vikram calendar
- Several Indian states use it in **official almanacs** and ceremonial proclamations

An emperor whose era has kept counting for more than two millennia is not folklore frozen in a book. He is the reason a shopkeeper in 2026 writes one year on a bill and reads another on the wall above the counter.

And the timekeeping suits the city perfectly. Ancient Indian astronomers treated Ujjain as the reference meridian of the land — the zero line from which longitude and time were reckoned. The **Vedh Shala** observatory still stands testimony to that heritage; our guide to the [Jantar Mantar and Vedh Shala in Ujjain](/blog/jantar-mantar-vedh-shala-ujjain/) explains the instruments you can still see.

## The Vikram Samvat by the numbers

To appreciate the scale of this legacy, here is a simple comparison:

| Calendar | Start year (traditional) | Current year (2026 CE) | Still in use? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gregorian | 1 CE | 2026 | Yes (global standard) |
| Vikram Samvat | 57 BCE | 2083 | Yes (India, Nepal) |
| Hijri | 622 CE | 1448 | Yes (Islamic world) |

The Vikram Samvat is 2083 in 2026 — fifty-seven years ahead of the Gregorian calendar. That gap is the king's legacy, still ticking forward.

## Vikramaditya's court: the golden age of Sanskrit learning

The legends do not stop at justice and military prowess. Vikramaditya's court is remembered as one of the great intellectual flowering points of ancient India — a magnet for poets, scholars, and philosophers who made Ujjain a rival to Pataliputra and Takshashila in the world of letters.

The tradition of the **Navaratnas** — nine gems of his court — names figures like the playwright **Kalidasa**, whose *Abhijnanashakuntalam* and *Meghaduta* remain cornerstones of Sanskrit drama, and **Amarasimha**, the lexicographer whose *Amarakosha* is still the standard Sanskrit thesaurus taught in universities today. Whether or not these nine scholars sat in one royal assembly, the legend points to something real: Ujjain under Vikramaditya became synonymous with Sanskrit excellence.

This is not merely ancient history. The tradition of scholarly Ujjain continues. The **Vikram Kirti Mandir** houses a manuscript library that preserves palm-leaf texts from centuries later, showing that the city's identity as a centre of learning persisted long after the king's era. Visitors often find that the quiet of the manuscript hall, with its smell of old paper and oil lamps, is one of the most moving experiences the city offers — a direct thread to the world Kalidasa walked.

## The Vedh Shala and Ujjain as India's astronomical capital

Long before Jai Singh built his famous Jantar Mantars, Ujjain was the astronomical reference point for the subcontinent. Because the city sits close to the Tropic of Cancer and ancient astronomers established it as the prime meridian for Indian timekeeping, **Ujjain was zero longitude** — the place from which all other calculations were measured.

The **Vedh Shala** observatory, built by Maharaja Jai Singh in the 1720s, stands on this ancient tradition. Its instruments — the samrat yantra, the digamsha yantra, the ram yantra — do not merely tell time. They encode a cosmology that begins here, in Ujjain, at the latitude where the sun turns directly overhead at summer solstice.

Walking the Vedh Shala at sunrise, when the shadow of the gnomon sweeps cleanly across the quadrant, you feel the continuity: the same sky, the same meridian, the same need to measure what is infinite that moved the king who gave his name to the calendar. ## Vikramaditya in popular culture: from baital tales to modern retellings

The Baital Pachisi and Simhasan Battisi are not dusty museum pieces. They are among the the most adapted story cycles in Indian literature, resurfacing in comics, television serials, graphic novels, and regional theatre every few decades. The 1980s Doordarshan serial *Vikram Aur Betaal* made the vetala tales a household event for an entire generation of Indians, and more recently web series and animated adaptations have found new audiences.

Why the endless return? The stories are about a ruler who is never above the test. Every riddle the vetala asks is a question of ethics that any listener, in any age, must answer for themselves. The Baital tales work because they do not offer easy moral lessons — they offer moral laboratories. You enter them the same way Vikramaditya enters the forest: without knowing what the next question will demand of you.

That quality is why the Vikramaditya legend survives where so many other royal legends have faded. A king remembered only for conquest is a figure of the past. A king remembered for the questions he kept answering is a figure who can still speak.

## Practical tips for the Vikramaditya heritage trail

If you want to trace the king's legacy across Ujjain in one day, here is a route that works:

1. **Start at Mahakaleshwar Temple (4 AM)** — Bhasma Aarti if registered, otherwise early morning darshan
2. **Vikram Kirti Mandir (8:30 AM)** — 45–60 minutes in the museum
3. **Vedh Shala (10:00 AM)** — 30 minutes at the observatory
4. **Ram Ghat (5:00 PM)** — evening aarti on the Shipra
5. **Dinner near Gopal Mandir Road** — pure veg, early enough for rest before the next morning

### Morning logistics

- **Wear comfortable closed footwear** for the temple and observatory walks; you will remove shoes multiple times
- **Carry a small water bottle** — Ujjain's heat between October and March is dry and deceptively tiring
- **Arrive at the temple before 4 AM** if you want a Bhasma Aarti seat; online booking opens 30 days in advance on the official portal
- **Vikram Kirti Mandir is closed Mondays** — plan around it if you are arriving on a weekend
- **Carry cash** for small donations and photo fees at heritage sites; card acceptance is inconsistent

### Photography etiquette

Photography is restricted inside the Mahakaleshwar Temple sanctum and at some Vikram Kirti Mandir manuscript displays. Ask the guard before raising your camera. The Vedh Shala grounds and Ram Ghat are freely photographable, especially at golden hour when the stone instruments glow warm against the sky.

## The Simhasan Battisi: a deeper reading

The Simhasan Battisi is not just a children's story — it is one of the great philosophical texts of Indian literature, disguised as entertainment. Each of the thirty-two statues tells a tale of Vikramaditya's rule, and each tale is a lesson in governance:

1. **Justice without bias** — the king who judged the poor and the rich equally
2. **Courage in adversity** — the king who faced danger without flinching
3. **Generosity to scholars** — the king who valued learning above all wealth
4. **Humility before the divine** — the king who knew his power had limits
5. **Patience in deliberation** — the king who never rushed a verdict

These themes are not ancient relics. They are the values the city still invokes when it calls Vikramaditya its own. Reading the stories alongside the city that produced them adds a layer of meaning that no textbook can convey.

## Walking his capital today

You do not need a museum to meet Vikramaditya. Walk **Gopal Mandir Road** at dawn and you pass his city in motion: shutters rolling up, pooja baskets being filled, milk pots heading for the temple, pilgrims murmuring mantras under their breath.

- Begin at the **Mahakaleshwar temple gate**, 200 m from our doorstep
- Drift through the temple quarter lanes, where old havelis stand shoulder to shoulder with sweet shops
- Continue to **Ram Ghat**, about 500 m away, for the evening Shipra aarti
- Glance at any panchang on a shop wall — the king's era, still counting

That last line is the whole story. In Ujjain, Vikramaditya is not behind you in history. He is printed above the shopkeeper's head, one era ahead of the modern world.

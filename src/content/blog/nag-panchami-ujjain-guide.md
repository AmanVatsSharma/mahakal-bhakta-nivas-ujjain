Ujjain keeps one door locked all year. On the third floor of the Mahakaleshwar temple, above the sanctum where the Bhasma Aarti smoulders each dawn, sits the shrine of Nagchandreshwar — and it opens for darshan on exactly one day: **Nag Panchami**. On that night the city changes shape. **Nag Panchami in Ujjain** is not simply snake worship; it is the one night when a shrine the whole country has read about but almost no one has seen unlocks its doors, and lakhs of devotees arrive to walk through them.

Here is what happens on that extraordinary day, and how to plan it if it finds you in the city.

## The Shrine That Sleeps All Year

The **Nagchandreshwar shrine** occupies the uppermost floor of the Mahakaleshwar complex. The image within shows the deity sheltered beneath the spread hood of a great serpent — a form so rarely displayed that tradition keeps it veiled for the whole year except this single day. **Nagchandreshwar darshan at Mahakaleshwar** is therefore unlike any other temple experience in India: not a faster queue or a better seat, but a door that is literally closed on the other 364 days.

The day itself is fixed by the lunar calendar — **Shravan Shukla Panchami**, falling in the monsoon. Tradition holds that the shrine opens around midnight and remains open until the following midnight, but the exact hours change with the temple's arrangements each year: verify at the temple counter before you plan the night.

The single-day opening is among the most storied customs of any temple in India — devotees plan whole years around it, and families who witness it once tend to speak of it for decades. Because the date is lunar, it never falls on the same Gregorian day twice; the panchang decides, and the wise traveller checks it before booking anything.

## A Night of Queues

Be clear-eyed about what the night asks. Devotees begin lining up hours before the doors move, and the queue winds through the temple corridors and out into the lanes, moving slowly through the dark. You will stand a long time — often several hours — among families with children asleep on shoulders, elders on folding stools, first-time visitors and veterans who come every single year. The **darshan itself is brief**, as such crowded darshans always are: a few steps, a glimpse, a heartbeat before the line carries you on. Ask anyone in that crowd and they will tell you it was worth it.

Practical notes from experience:

- Carry **water and something light to eat**; the wait outlasts dinner
- Keep phones charged, but expect the network to jam near the gate
- Wear comfortable footwear you can stand in for hours
- If you are bringing elders, many devotees bring a light folding stool — and honestly judge their stamina before committing them to the night

## What to Carry for the Night

- Water bottles and light, dry snacks — the stalls near the gate stay open, but the queue does not pause for them
- A fully charged phone and a power bank; the network near the temple sags under the crowd
- Comfortable footwear and a thin shawl, since the wait runs through the coolest hours of the night
- A folding stool for elders, and a clear agreement on where the group meets if separated
- Patience, above all — the night is long precisely because the darshan is rare

## Snake Worship Across the City

Nag Panchami itself is older and wider than the one shrine. In homes across Ujjain, families offer milk and flowers to images of the serpent, and many traditionally avoid digging the earth or frying food on the day — customs of respect for the nag, who is believed to be close to the surface in Shravan. Through the day, smaller nag shrines around the city draw their own quiet circles of devotees, and the connection runs deep in this region, where serpent worship and the Kaal Sarp shanti traditions of Ujjain lean on the same devotional root.

The difference between a bearable night and an exhausting one is almost entirely the room:
